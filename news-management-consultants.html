How Management Consultants Can Merge Expectations & Manage Resistance With Clients: Best Practices

Home›News›Management Consultants
← Back to News

Generally, the main obligations of management consultants to their clients are to assist the client to solve management or operational issues, create value for its customers and stakeholders, reach new markets and sustain their position in them, and strengthen their overall competitiveness. Delivering on these obligations depends on one thing above all others: how well the consultant manages expectations from the very first conversation.

Start With What "Done" Looks Like

It is easy for an engagement to start with both sides nodding along to the same words — "efficiency," "growth," "transformation" — while picturing very different outcomes. Before any work begins, we agree in writing on the specific deliverables, the timeline, and just as importantly, what sits outside the scope of the engagement. A client who understands the boundary of what they're paying for is far less likely to feel short-changed later, and a consultant who has defined that boundary is far less likely to quietly absorb scope creep that erodes the quality of everything else.

Expect Resistance — And Plan For It

Change, even good change, threatens something: a routine, a reporting line, a sense of control. Resistance rarely shows up as outright refusal; more often it looks like delayed responses, meetings that don't quite happen, or quiet non-adoption of a new process once the consultant has left the building. The mistake we see most often is treating resistance as a problem to overcome at the end of a project, rather than a signal to plan for at the start.

The people most likely to resist are usually the people closest to the work being changed — middle managers, department heads, long-serving staff. Bringing them into the design of a solution, rather than presenting them with a finished one, converts a lot of that resistance into ownership before it has a chance to calcify into opposition.

Credibility is not built by promising the ideal outcome. It is built by delivering the realistic one, on time, every time.

Three Practices We Apply at Aliska

  • We co-create the engagement brief with the client's leadership team before any work begins, rather than handing over a proposal written in isolation.
  • We schedule short, regular check-ins throughout the engagement instead of saving everything for one long report at the end — surprises are far easier to manage in small doses.
  • We document decisions and trade-offs as they're made, not reconstructed afterward from memory, so there's a clear record of why the project went the way it did.

When Engagements End

How a consultant leaves matters almost as much as how they arrive. We give clients advance notice before an engagement winds down, and we build in a short handover period — documentation, training for internal staff, and a defined window for follow-up questions — rather than finishing a report and disappearing. A graceful exit is often what determines whether a client calls you again for the next project.

Managing expectations isn't a soft skill bolted onto the real work of consulting — for us, it is the real work. Every technical recommendation we make is only as good as the client's willingness and ability to act on it, and that willingness is built, conversation by conversation, long before the final report is delivered.
